# How should end-to-end processes be mapped and modeled?

> Source: https://www.apqc.org/How-should-end-to-end-processes-be-mapped-and-modeled
> Collected: 2026-09-21
> Published: 2026-09

## Define the Scope and Boundaries

Before creating the map, the team should identify the business outcome or goal, the event that starts the process, the result that ends the process, the process environment, key stakeholders and their needs, boundaries, constraints, resource limitations, and connections with upstream and downstream processes. Clear boundaries prevent process overlap and keep the mapping effort focused.

## Map the Entire Cross-Functional Flow

A high-level map should trace the process from beginning to end and show how work crosses functional boundaries. It should make handoffs between departments visible and connect all participating groups to the same outcome. Mapping horizontally helps reveal delays, duplication, unclear ownership, conflicting practices, and gaps that may be hidden when each function documents only its own work.

## Document the Current State

A current-state or “as-is” model should reflect how work is actually performed. It should capture the process steps, roles, systems, rules, handoffs, and variations currently in use.

The current-state model creates a baseline for identifying inconsistencies, risks, bottlenecks, unnecessary steps, and improvement opportunities.

## Design the Future State

A future-state or “to-be” model should show how the process is intended to operate after improvement. Teams should use current-state evidence, stakeholder requirements, business goals, and performance gaps to determine what should change.

Future-state models can address redesigned activities, clarified responsibilities, standardized handoffs, revised business rules, technology changes, or new performance controls.

## Validate and Measure

Process owners, stewards, workers, subject-matter experts, upstream and downstream stakeholders, and the BPM team should review the map. Validation should confirm that scope and boundaries are correct, activities reflect actual or intended work, roles and handoffs are accurate, dependencies are visible, terminology is understood, and improvement assumptions are feasible.

Measures should reveal whether the end-to-end process achieves its purpose. Common categories include cost effectiveness, staff productivity, process efficiency, and cycle time. A focused set is generally more useful than a crowded dashboard.
